The Language of Climate Justice
8 min

By the end of this section, you'll have language for what you've always sensed — but couldn't quite name.

Five concepts. Five lenses.

In Section 1, you started noticing. Now let's name what you saw. These five concepts give language to patterns that many people feel but struggle to articulate — and they'll be your lenses for everything that follows.

Explore each card. Flip it to see what the concept means and why it matters. We'll put them all together at the end.

Tree Equity
Tap to explore →
What it means
The fair distribution of urban trees and green spaces across all neighborhoods — regardless of income, race, or location. Everyone has a voice in decisions about their community's green spaces.
Why it matters
Trees cool the air by up to 10°C on hot days, filter pollution, reduce flooding, and improve mental health. When some neighborhoods have trees and others don't, the health gap is real.
In Pawtucket, a free tree-planting program existed — but only residents of already-green neighborhoods applied. The people who needed trees most didn't know it existed.
⚖️
Environmental Racism
Tap to explore →
What it means
The pattern where racialized communities disproportionately bear environmental burdens — pollution, extreme heat, lack of green space — while having less power over decisions that affect their environment.
Why it matters
This isn't accidental. It's the result of decades of policies — redlining, highway placement, zoning laws — that concentrated environmental harm in specific communities. History shaped who is most vulnerable today.
Across five Canadian cities, Nature Canada found that low-income and racialized neighborhoods consistently had less tree canopy. Not by chance. By design.
🔗
Intersectionality
Tap to explore →
What it means
A framework for understanding how different aspects of identity — race, income, gender, disability, age — overlap and combine to shape people's experiences of climate harm.
Why it matters
Climate change doesn't affect everyone equally. A low-income elderly person with a disability faces different — and often greater — risks during a heat wave than a young, wealthy, able-bodied person.
During the 2021 BC heat dome, most deaths occurred among elderly people living alone, without AC, in low-income neighborhoods with less tree cover. Age, income, housing, and neighborhood all intersected.
🌡️
Climate Apartheid
Tap to explore →
What it means
A world where the wealthy can pay to escape the worst impacts of climate change — moving to cooler areas, buying air conditioning — while those with fewer resources suffer consequences they did the least to cause.
Why it matters
The communities that contributed most to the problem often have the most resources to adapt. Those who contributed least are most exposed and least able to cope. That's not a side effect — it's a justice crisis.
The UN Special Rapporteur on extreme poverty warned of a scenario where the wealthy pay to escape overheating, hunger, and conflict while the rest of the world is left to suffer.
🪶
Environmental Colonialism
Tap to explore →
What it means
Patterns where powerful groups make decisions about land, resources, and environments without the consent — or against the interests — of the people who live there. It connects historical colonialism to ongoing environmental injustices.
Why it matters
In Canada, Indigenous communities were displaced from their lands and continue to face resource extraction imposed without meaningful consent. Climate solutions that ignore this history risk repeating it.
The Nature Canada report calls for decolonizing urban forestry by centering Indigenous communities as partners and knowledge-holders in greening initiatives.

These five concepts don't exist in isolation. In real communities, they overlap and compound — the same neighborhood can experience all of them at once.
